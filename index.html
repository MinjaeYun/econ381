Course Overview

This course is designed to equip students with the foundational knowledge of probability and statistical inference necessary to read, understand, and apply econometric studies with rigor and confidence.

It provides foundational training in probability and statistics for graduate-level econometrics. Weekly problem sets develop technical fluency and critical thinking to assess and critique empirical research in economics and related fields.

  • Credit: 4.0 units
  • Meetings: one 2 hour 50 minute session per week (Wednesdays), 16 weeks

Course Learning Outcomes

Upon successful completion of this course, students will be able to:

  1. Understand and interpret fundamental statistical concepts and their associated mathematical notation.
  2. Apply these concepts to read and make sense of econometric studies, especially those involving formal models and equations.
  3. Analyze econometric applications by connecting theory to empirical methods.
  4. Evaluate the methodological soundness of applied econometric work, laying the groundwork for future research.

Background Preparation

Students should have a solid foundation in undergraduate calculus (Calculus I and II), including multivariable differentiation and integration. Familiarity with basic linear algebra is essential. Prior exposure to introductory probability and statistics is recommended.

Recommended Course Materials

The primary lecture material is based on Casella and Berger; Wackerly et al. also provides extensive coverage of the necessary topics.

  • Casella, G., & Berger, R. L. Statistical Inference (2nd ed.).
  • Wackerly, D. D., Mendenhall, W., & Scheaffer, R. L. Mathematical Statistics with Applications (7th ed.).

Selected topics are also referenced from:

  • Wasserman, L. All of Statistics: A Concise Course in Statistical Inference. Springer.
  • Bierens, H. J. Introduction to the Mathematical and Statistical Foundations of Econometrics.

Course Format

  • Lecture notes and homework were distributed through Canvas.
  • Homework was assigned weekly; students attempted problems independently before working through them in weekly TA sessions.
  • Midterm and final exams were held in person.
